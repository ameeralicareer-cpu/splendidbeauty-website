Thinning vs Texturising Scissors: What Is the Difference?

Cutting, thinning and texturising scissors explained for barbers and hairdressers in the UK.

The names get mixed up, and some shops use them as if they mean the same thing. They do not. All three do a different job, and most professionals use more than one. Here is the simple version.

Cutting scissors

Cutting scissors have two plain blades. You use them to set the length, shape the cut and make clean lines. They are the main scissor in your kit.

Thinning scissors

Thinning scissors have one plain blade and one blade with fine, close teeth. Each closing only cuts some of the hair, so they take out bulk and blend lengths without changing the overall length. They are the scissor for softening a line or removing weight from thick hair.

Texturising scissors

Texturising scissors also have a toothed blade, but the teeth are usually wider apart. They remove more hair in each cut and leave gaps, which creates movement, separation and a visible texture. They are used for point cutting effects, breaking up solid lines and adding shape to the ends.

Quick comparison

  • Cutting: plain blades. Sets length and shape.
  • Thinning: fine, close teeth. Removes bulk and blends smoothly.
  • Texturising: wider teeth. Removes more hair and creates visible texture.

Names vary between brands. Some call a fine-toothed scissor a texturiser and some call a wide-toothed one a thinner, so always look at the teeth rather than the name. As a rule, more teeth set closer together blend more softly, and fewer, wider teeth take more hair out.

Which one do you need?

  • Thick hair that feels heavy: thinning scissors to remove weight.
  • Harsh lines after a cut: thinning scissors to blend them.
  • Flat ends that need movement: texturising scissors.
  • Everything else: a good cutting scissor first. A thinning or texturising scissor is an addition, not a replacement.

Tips for using them

Use toothed scissors on dry or slightly damp hair, start with a few cuts and check the result, and keep away from the root unless you want a very short, spiky effect. It is easy to take out too much, so go slowly. Keep the blades clean and oiled, see how to look after your scissors.

Common questions

Are thinning scissors and texturising scissors the same? No. Both have teeth, but thinning scissors usually have finer, closer teeth for blending, and texturising scissors have wider teeth that remove more hair and create texture.

Can I use thinning scissors to cut hair to length? No. They only remove part of the hair, so use cutting scissors for length.

Do I need both? Many professionals keep a cutting scissor and one toothed scissor. Which toothed scissor depends on the finish you want.
